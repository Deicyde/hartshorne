# Hartshorne IV.4, elliptic curves

The primary source is the repository PDF, *Algebraic Geometry* (1977),
Chapter IV, §4. The section begins on printed p.316. Its running text occupies
pp.316–336, Exercises 4.1–4.17 occupy pp.336–339, and Exercises 4.18–4.22
continue on p.340 immediately before §5. Thus the complete source boundary is
printed pp.316–340, not pp.316–339.

Hartshorne works over a fixed algebraically closed field `k` and omits
characteristic two. An elliptic curve is a Chapter-IV curve of genus one
together with a chosen point `P_0`. The scheme, its set of closed points, a
Weierstrass equation, and Mathlib's explicit `WeierstrassCurve` point types
must remain distinct until comparison theorems are proved.

## The j-invariant and Legendre form, printed pp.317–321

The degree-two system `|2P_0|` maps an elliptic curve to `P^1`, ramified at
four points. In characteristic different from two, normalize the branch set
to `0,1,lambda,infinity` and define

`j(lambda) = 2^8 * (lambda^2-lambda+1)^3 /
  (lambda^2*(lambda-1)^2)`.

Theorem 4.1 states that this is independent of all choices, two elliptic
curves have the same `j` exactly when they are isomorphic, and every element
of `k` occurs.

Lemma 4.2 constructs an involution interchanging any two specified points;
Corollary 4.3 makes the automorphism group transitive. Lemma 4.4 compares any
two degree-two maps by automorphisms of source and target. Lemma 4.5 computes
the six-element `S_3` orbit of `lambda`.

Proposition 4.6 embeds `(X,P_0)` by `|3P_0|`, first obtaining a general
Weierstrass equation and then, in characteristic not two, the Legendre form

`y^2 = x(x-1)(x-lambda)`.

This proves Theorem 4.1. The examples compute `j=1728` for `y^2=x^3-x` and
`j=0` for the Fermat cubic. Corollary 4.7 gives the order of
`Aut(X,P_0)`: normally two, four at `j=1728`, six at `j=0`, and twelve when
`j=0=1728` in characteristic three.

## Group law and endomorphisms, printed pp.321–323

The bijection `P |-> O_X(P-P_0)` transports the group law of `Pic^0 X` to
the points of `X`; in the plane cubic embedding, three collinear points sum
to zero. Proposition 4.8 proves inversion and addition are morphisms, so the
point group is a group variety. Multiplication by every integer is therefore
a morphism.

The text computes the two- and three-torsion in characteristics prime to two
and three and records that multiplication by `n` has degree `n^2` with kernel
`(Z/nZ)^2` when `n` is prime to the characteristic. Lemma 4.9 says every
origin-preserving morphism of elliptic curves is a group homomorphism. The
origin-preserving endomorphisms form a ring, and Proposition 4.10 embeds
`Z` in it and proves every nonzero multiplication map is finite.

Exercise 4.6 and Exercise 4.7 supply the later general torsion-degree and
dual-isogeny arguments used by these statements.

## Picard functors and the Jacobian, printed pp.323–326

For a finite-type `k`-scheme `T`, Hartshorne defines relative degree-zero
Picard classes by quotienting line bundles on `X times T`, fibrewise of
degree zero, by pullbacks from `T`. A Jacobian represents this functor.
Assuming representability, the universal class makes it a group scheme; its
closed points are `Pic^0 X`, its tangent space at zero is `H^1(X,O_X)`, and
it is proper. Symmetric-power Abel maps show it is irreducible of dimension
`g` and regular.

The general existence assertion is cited to Chow, Mumford, and Grothendieck
and is a source obligation. Theorem 4.11 proves the elliptic case directly:
`J=X`, with the normalized diagonal bundle as Poincare bundle. Cohomology and
base change produces the unique section whose graph gives the classifying
map. The resulting group law agrees with the earlier divisor-class law.

## Elliptic functions and complex uniformization, printed pp.326–332

Hartshorne imports analytic results, marked `B`, from Hurwitz–Courant.
For a lattice `Lambda=Z+Z*tau`, Theorem 4.12B says the elliptic-function
field is generated by the Weierstrass functions `wp,wp'`, satisfying

`wp'^2 = 4*wp^3-g_2*wp-g_3`.

The induced map `C/Lambda` is a bijective analytic uniformization of the
corresponding nonsingular cubic. Theorem 4.13B is Abel's divisor criterion:
`sum n_i(a_i)` is principal exactly when both its degree and group sum vanish.
Theorem 4.14B realizes every nonsingular complex Weierstrass cubic by a
lattice. Theorem 4.15B classifies equal modular invariants under the
`GL_2(Z)` fractional-linear action and chooses a fundamental-domain
representative.

Consequently a complex elliptic curve is `R/Z times R/Z` as an abstract
group, multiplication by `n` has degree `n^2`, and its endomorphisms are
lattice multipliers. The converse direction in Proposition 4.18 uses GAGA to
turn a holomorphic quotient homomorphism into an algebraic morphism.
Proposition 4.19 classifies complex multiplication by imaginary-quadratic
orders; Corollary 4.20 gives countably many CM `j`-values. The Gaussian,
Eisenstein, and nonmaximal-order examples follow.

Remark 4.20.4 quotes deeper CM class-field facts: `j` is integral,
`K(j)/K` is abelian of degree the order's class number, and precisely thirteen
integral `j`-values have class number one. These require a separate
proof-level source.

## The Hasse invariant, printed pp.332–335

Absolute Frobenius induces a `p`-semilinear endomorphism of the
one-dimensional space `H^1(X,O_X)`. Hasse invariant zero means this map is
zero; otherwise it is bijective. Proposition 4.21 computes it for a plane
cubic: it vanishes exactly when the coefficient of `(xyz)^(p-1)` in
`f^(p-1)` vanishes. Corollary 4.22 specializes to the Legendre Hasse
polynomial. Corollary 4.23 bounds the number of supersingular `j`-values by
`floor(p/12)+2`.

The small-prime examples are direct calculations. The following note quotes
Igusa for squarefreeness of the Hasse polynomial and the exact count. The CM
reduction remark quotes Deuring and states density one half in the CM case;
in the non-CM case it states density zero, Elkies's infinitude theorem, and
the Lang–Trotter asymptotic as a conjecture. These are external theorem or
conjecture nodes, not elementary consequences of the Hasse polynomial.

The `j=1728` example proves supersingularity exactly for primes
`p congruent 3 mod 4`; a second non-CM example records the first small
supersingular prime.

## Rational points, printed pp.335–336

When a pointed plane model is defined over a subfield `k_0`, its `k_0`-points
form a subgroup. Hartshorne quotes Mordell's theorem that `X(Q)` is finitely
generated, then gives the Fermat cubic with three rational points and the
curve `y^2+y=x^3-x`, whose rational points Tate identifies as infinite
cyclic. These quoted arithmetic results require their cited proof sources.

and algebraic group-law arguments are not blocked merely because they are

## Pinned Mathlib audit and false friends

The pinned checkout is commit
`0df444a360eaa60ab8c11dca51a86af692955474` (`v4.33.1`). Exact supporting
results include `WeierstrassCurve.variableChange_j`,
`exists_variableChange_of_j_eq`, `ofJ_j`, normal-form transformations,
explicit affine/projective/Jacobian-coordinate point formulae, two-torsion
and division polynomials, and `PeriodPair.derivWeierstrassP_sq`.

The last declaration is an exact match for the differential-equation clause
of Theorem 4.12B when isolated as its own leaf. The pinned analytic API also
defines `wp,wp'` and proves periodicity, meromorphicity, and analyticity, but
not field generation, quotient uniformization, Abel's criterion, lattice
existence, or the modular fundamental-domain theorem.

Mathlib's `WeierstrassCurve` and its point types are coefficient and point
models, not the adopted smooth proper scheme with a chosen origin. Its
`WeierstrassCurve.Jacobian` namespace means weighted Jacobian coordinates,
not the Picard/Jacobian variety. Pointwise group-law formulae are not proofs
that addition is a scheme morphism; division polynomials are not finite
kernel group schemes or dual isogenies; reduction and L-function files do not
define the Hasse map on `H^1(O_X)`. Thus no scheme-level IV.4 theorem is
marked exact merely from these APIs.

Recent overlapping work includes Andrew Yang's Weierstrass-function series
(PRs #31294, #32089, #32911, #33723), Jz Pan's `j`/variable-change
classification, and David Kurniadi Angdinata and Michael Stoll's explicit
Weierstrass point/formula API.
